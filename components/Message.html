Why this exists

I love graph rewriting and procedural generation: write a few rules that swap one thing for another, apply them over and over, and shapes turn up that nobody drew.

What pulls me in isn't symmetry but reduction to primitives: a tiny set of atoms and strict rules that build everything else, known as compositionality or a minimal generating set. Psychology calls that kind of mind systemizing; in games, it's the systems-design instinct. This page works the same way: one triangle, two make a rectangle, and that rectangle, turned, draws every line of every letter.

Graph rewriting swaps one piece of a graph for another. John Pfaltz and Azriel Rosenfeld were among the first to do it, in 1969, on labelled graphs they called webs, and its rule sets became graph grammars. In 1973 Hartmut Ehrig, Michael Pfender and Hans-Jürgen Schneider gave it the algebraic form much of the field still builds on. Maxim Gumin's wave function collapse learns which pieces may sit side by side from a single example, then fills a grid that only follows what it learned.

This site borrows from all three. A–Z is the example: every line a new letter draws must sit where A–Z already has one. A grammar rewrites a glyph into parts and parts into pieces, joint rules settle where parts meet, and anything that breaks a rule is drawn again.
