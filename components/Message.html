Why this exists

I love graph rewriting and procedural generation. Write down a few rules that swap one thing for another, apply them over and over, and shapes turn up that nobody drew.

What pulls me in isn't really symmetry. It's reduction to primitives: a tiny set of atoms and strict rules that build everything else, usually called compositionality or a minimal generating set. Psychology calls a mind that works this way systemizing, and in games it's the systems-design instinct. It's how I think, and this page is built the same way: one triangle, two of them make a rectangle, and that rectangle, turned, draws every line of every letter.

Graph rewriting means finding one piece of a graph and swapping in another. John Pfaltz and Azriel Rosenfeld were among the first to do it, in 1969, on labelled graphs they called webs, and the rule sets that do it became graph grammars. In 1973 Hartmut Ehrig, Michael Pfender and Hans-Jürgen Schneider gave graph rewriting the algebraic form much of the field still builds on. Wave function collapse by Maxim Gumin learns which pieces may sit next to each other from a single example, then fills a grid with output that only ever follows what it learned.

This site borrows from all three. A–Z is the example: every line a new letter draws has to sit somewhere A–Z already uses. A grammar rewrites a glyph into parts and its parts into pieces, joint rules settle where the parts meet, and anything that breaks a rule is thrown away and drawn again.
