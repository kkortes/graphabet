Why this exists

I love rewriting. Write down a few rules that swap one thing for another, apply them over and over, and shapes turn up that nobody drew.

Rewriting a graph means finding one piece of it and swapping in another. John Pfaltz and Azriel Rosenfeld were among the first to do it, in 1969, on labelled graphs they called webs, and the rule sets that do it became graph grammars. In 1973 Hartmut Ehrig, Michael Pfender and Hans-Jürgen Schneider gave graph rewriting the algebraic form much of the field still builds on. Wave function collapse by Maxim Gumin learns which pieces may sit next to each other from a single example, then fills a grid with output that only ever follows what it learned.

This site borrows from all three. A–Z is the example: every line a new letter draws has to sit somewhere A–Z already uses. A grammar rewrites a glyph into parts and its parts into pieces, joint rules settle where the parts meet, and anything that breaks a rule is thrown away and drawn again.
